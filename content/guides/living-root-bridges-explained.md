A living root bridge is the only piece of infrastructure we know of that gets stronger the longer you leave it alone. That single fact explains why Khasi villages have grown them for generations, and why travellers walk thousands of steps to stand on one.

This guide covers how the bridges work, where to see them, how hard each walk is, and what their UNESCO status actually means.

## What a root bridge is made of

Every living root bridge in Meghalaya starts with one species: the rubber fig, *Ficus elastica*. It is the same plant sold as a houseplant in cities, but in the wet forests of the Khasi and Jaintia hills it grows into a large tree that drops aerial roots from its trunk and branches.

Those aerial roots are the building material. They are soft and pliable when young, they keep growing towards soil and moisture, and, importantly, they fuse where they touch. Botanists call this inosculation. Two roots pressed together for long enough grow into one, and a mesh of crossing roots becomes a single rigid lattice.

The trees also like where the bridges are needed. Rubber figs root happily on rocky riverbanks and hold on through floods that would wash away a timber post.

## How villagers grow one

There is no single method. Builders in different villages use what works for their stream. The common steps look like this:

1. **Choose or plant a fig** on one bank, sometimes on both.
2. **Guide young roots across** using a temporary frame. The traditional guide is the hollowed trunk of an areca (betel nut) palm split lengthwise and laid over the stream; roots grow along it and the decaying trunk feeds them. Bamboo scaffolds and, more recently, steel cables are also used.
3. **Anchor the roots on the far bank**, where they enter the soil and start drawing nutrients.
4. **Weave, tie and add more roots** over the years to form handrails, side walls and a deck.
5. **Fill the deck with stones and soil** so people walk on a level surface while the roots below keep thickening.

A bridge usually becomes safe to walk on after roughly 10 to 15 years, and longer spans take more. After that, maintenance is steady but light: replacing loose deck stones, redirecting new roots, and trimming growth that pulls the span out of shape.

| Stage | Typical timing | What is happening |
|---|---|---|
| Planting and guiding | Year 0 to 3 | Fig established, first roots guided along areca trunks or bamboo |
| Anchoring | Year 3 to 8 | Roots reach the far bank and root into soil |
| First crossings | About year 10 to 15 | Roots fused enough to carry a person; deck filled with stones |
| Mature bridge | Decades onward | Roots thicken, spans can carry many people at once |
| Renewal | Ongoing | New roots added, damaged sections rerouted by the community |

These are indicative ranges we have heard from builders and guides, not fixed rules. A healthy tree on a narrow stream can move faster; a wide span can take a generation.

## Why the Khasi hills grow bridges

The southern slopes of the Khasi Hills catch the full monsoon off the Bay of Bengal. [Sohra](/places/sohra/) and neighbouring Mawsynram are among the wettest inhabited places on earth, with annual rainfall measured in metres rather than centimetres. In that climate, cut timber rots within a few seasons and flash floods take out anything rigid.

A living bridge solves both problems. It does not rot because it is alive, and its roots flex with floodwater rather than snapping. For villages in deep valleys, such as Nongriat, these bridges were the difference between reaching the market and being cut off for the rains.

Researchers, including teams working on "living architecture" in Germany, have studied the bridges as examples of structures that repair and strengthen themselves. The larger point is simpler: the knowledge is local, oral and still in use.

## Three myths worth clearing up

A few claims turn up again and again on social media. Here is what we tell travellers.

**"They are 500 years old."** Some may be old, but there is no reliable dating for most bridges. The tradition is oral, and villagers talk about who started or restored a bridge, not a founding year. The often-quoted figure for the Nongriat double decker is around 200 years, and even that is an estimate.

**"They are a dying art."** Not quite. Several villages have started new bridges in recent decades, and local groups and young builders have worked to document and restore older ones. The bigger risk is not neglect but overuse at a handful of famous spans while bridges in less-known villages see few visitors.

**"There is only one double decker."** The Umshiang bridge at Nongriat is the famous one, but multi-level and ladder-like root structures exist in other villages too. What makes Nongriat special is the combination of the stacked spans, the village setting and the swimming pools downstream.

## Bridges and village life

It helps to remember that these are working bridges first and attractions second. Children walk across them to school, farmers carry betel nut, bay leaf and broom grass over them to the road, and families maintain the section nearest their land. In Nongriat, where there is still no motorable road, almost everything, from rice sacks to gas cylinders, reaches the village on someone's back via the same steps you walk.

That shapes how a visit feels. You will pass homes, small shops selling tea, biscuits and cold drinks, and signs asking visitors to keep noise down. The entry fees you pay go to the village, and the homestays are family-run. Spending a night, buying a meal and hiring a local guide for Rainbow Falls puts money directly into the community that keeps the bridges alive.

## The UNESCO status, explained

This is where many articles get it wrong.

In 2022, India added **"Jingkieng Jri: Living Root Bridge Cultural Landscapes"** to its UNESCO World Heritage tentative list. The listing covers the living root bridge tradition across about 70 villages in East Khasi Hills and West Jaintia Hills, not only the famous double decker.

A tentative list is a country's inventory of sites it intends to nominate. It is a necessary first step, but it is not inscription. A full nomination still has to be prepared, evaluated and approved. Until the World Heritage Committee inscribes the site, the bridges are not a World Heritage Site, and anyone who tells you otherwise is overstating it. Check current status before you travel if you are writing about it or planning around it.

There are many more bridges than most itineraries include. These are the ones travellers reach most often.

Walking times vary with fitness and weather. Village entry and parking fees are small and paid in cash at the gate.

## The Nongriat double decker in detail

The [double decker root bridge at Nongriat](/places/nongriat-root-bridges/) is the one on every postcard: two spans stacked one above the other across the Umshiang stream, often estimated at around 200 years old. A third, higher level has been in training in recent years.

The walk starts at Tyrna, about 30 minutes by road below Sohra. From there, concrete steps drop steeply through village gardens, past areca and jackfruit trees, and cross two steel suspension bridges over the river. Most people take 2 to 3 hours down, stopping for photographs, and 2.5 to 3.5 hours back up.

If you have the legs, stay a night in Nongriat's simple homestays. That lets you walk another hour or so to Rainbow Falls, swim in the natural pools in the dry season and climb out the next morning while it is cool. ## Riwai and the easy option

Not everyone can manage 3,000 steps each way. The single root bridge at Riwai, next to [Mawlynnong](/places/mawlynnong/), is the answer. A short, stepped path leads down through forest to a compact bridge over a clear stream. Children and seniors manage it comfortably, and it slots into a day that includes Mawlynnong village and the Umngot river at Dawki.

The monsoon is when the bridges look most alive, and also when they are hardest to reach. If you go in July or August, wear shoes with real grip, start early and accept that the stream may be too high for swimming. ## How to visit without harming them

The villages own these bridges and depend on them. A few habits help:

- **Stay on the deck.** Do not climb the handrail roots or hang off side roots for photographs.
- **Leave the moss.** It protects the roots from drying out.
- **Respect crowd limits.** Some villages restrict how many people stand on a span at once. Wait your turn.
- **Carry plastic out.** Nongriat has no road, so everything that goes down has to be carried up.
- **Pay entry fees willingly.** They fund maintenance and village work.
- **Ask before photographing people.** Homes and gardens line the trail.
